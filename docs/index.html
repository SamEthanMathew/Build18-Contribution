LOLA is a mecanum-drive rover on a Raspberry Pi 4. A spinning LiDAR feeds a scan-matching SLAM front end that builds a 30 mm occupancy grid in real time; a phone-grade RGB camera feeds a transformer that rebuilds the same room as a coloured 3D point cloud. No depth sensor. No ROS. Total parts cost, $154.74.

How it fits together

Two loops that never touch the same wire

LOLA solves the room twice, in two independent passes. The geometry loop runs live at walking pace and answers where am I and what is solid. The appearance pass runs later, off the robot, and answers what does this room actually look like. They share a camera mount and nothing else.

Stage by stage

10 bytes

per LiDAR reading, over the wire

Getting the data off a Raspberry Pi that cannot keep up

A Pi 4 can spin the LiDAR and drive four motors, but scan matching on top of that starves the serial buffer and the map tears. So the Pi stops being the brain and becomes a sensor head: lidar_sender.py forwards every raw reading to a laptop over TCP and does nothing else with it.

The frame is deliberately dumb — one sync byte, then a packed quality/angle/distance triple. The receiver hunts for 0xA5 one byte at a time, so a dropped packet costs a single reading instead of resyncing the whole stream. 

// lidar_sender.py — Pi side
payload = b'\xa5' + struct.pack('<Bff', quality, angle, distance)
sock.sendall(payload)

// LiDARSensor.py — laptop side, resync on every frame
while True:
    sync = self.conn.recv(1)
    if sync == b'\xa5': break

A new revolution is detected by watching the angle wrap backwards by more than 100°, which is cheaper and more reliable than trusting a start-of-scan flag over a lossy link.

4 scans

merged before the pose is allowed to move

Knowing where you are, without an odometer

LOLA has no wheel encoders and no IMU. Every millimetre of its trajectory is inferred by aligning the current LiDAR scan against the previous one. A single A1M8 revolution is too sparse to align reliably, so four revolutions are stacked into one dense keyframe, then thinned to 400 points to keep the match interactive.

The alignment is point-to-line ICP. Ordinary ICP drags a point toward the nearest measured point, which fights you along a flat wall where the true correspondence is somewhere in between. PL-ICP estimates a surface normal from each point's six neighbours by PCA, projects onto that surface, and lets the scan slide freely along the wall while still being pinned across it. Corridors stop pulling the pose backwards.

A gate then throws away any motion under 20 mm or 2°. Without it the map quietly smears itself into mush while the rover sits still.

30 mm

one cell of the world

Turning returns into a floor plan

The map is a plain Python dictionary keyed by 30 mm cell. For each return, a Bresenham line is walked from the rover to the hit point: every cell along the way is pushed toward free, and only the endpoint is pushed toward occupied. Absence of evidence gets recorded as evidence of absence, which is what makes the rooms come out as rooms instead of outlines.

On export, morphological closing bridges the speckle gaps between returns so walls read as walls. The subtle part is the mask that follows: any cell already observed as free is forbidden from being closed over. Without that, the algorithm helpfully seals every doorway the rover just drove through.

5 000 iters

of random tree growth per plan

Choosing somewhere to go

The exported PNG is read straight back in as a planning surface: anything brighter than 200 is drivable, then eroded by 5 px so the rover does not clip a doorframe it is entitled to fit through. An RRT grows from the centre of the largest open region in 30 px steps, biased toward sampled goals one time in ten.

Waypoints are picked from the tree's leaves, furthest first, rejecting any that land too near one already chosen — a cheap way to force coverage instead of a huddle. A shortcut pass then deletes every waypoint the rover could have driven straight past.

Rebuilding the room from ordinary video

The occupancy grid is a floor plan — it knows where the walls are and nothing about what is on them. For that, LOLA records plain RGB video and hands it to CUT3R, a transformer that carries a persistent 3D state across frames and predicts depth, confidence and camera pose per frame. There is no checkerboard, no intrinsics file, and no depth sensor anywhere in the build.

Frames come out at 2 fps, get inferred at 512 px, then Open3D back-projects each depth map, drops anything past 10 m or under 0.30 confidence, moves it into world space with the predicted pose, and voxel-downsamples the union to a 1 cm cloud.

Two upstream patches are baked into the notebook and were the difference between working and not: swapping a deprecated tokens.type() in the CUROPE CUDA kernel, and forcing weights_only=False so the checkpoint still loads under PyTorch 2.6.

Printed parts

Sensor mounts and a replacement base, printed on a Bambu Lab X1 in PLA. Both are modelled to print flat with no supports, because the week did not include time for support removal. The STLs ship in the repository.

Why mecanum

Omnidirectional wheels let the rover strafe sideways without rotating. For a scan matcher with no odometry that is a real advantage: translation-only moves are far easier to resolve than a rotation that changes every correspondence at once.

Honest status

What works today, and what is still a wire

Seven days is seven days. Keeping this list accurate is more useful to anyone trying to rebuild LOLA than a claim of full autonomy would be.

Demonstrated

  • LiDAR streaming from the Pi to a laptop over TCP, with byte-level resync
  • PL-ICP scan matching, pose tracking and live occupancy mapping
  • Map export to PNG with gap closing that respects observed free space
  • RRT planning over an exported map, with waypoints in millimetres
  • End-to-end CUT3R reconstruction from a handheld clip to a fused point cloud
  • Keyboard teleop over all four mecanum wheels

Still a wire

  • Closing the drive loop. The laptop already serves a turn command on port 12346 and the Pi already asks for it — but the reply is printed to the terminal for a person to read. Feeding it into the motor calls is the next commit.
  • Putting waypoints in the world frame. Planner output is measured from the top-left of the exported PNG, not the SLAM origin. The crop offset is known at export time and simply is not written into the JSON yet.
  • Loop closure. Pose comes from consecutive scan matching alone, so long traverses drift. Revisiting a known place does not yet correct the map.

Run it yourself

Three commands and a notebook

# on the Pi — become a sensor head
python3 lidar_sender.py --host <laptop-ip>

# on the laptop — receive, match, map. press s to export a PNG
python LiDARSensor.py --network

# anywhere — plan a route over an exported map
python path_planner.py --input map_exports/slam_map_20260115-221847.png --num-waypoints 5

For the 3D pass, open Vidto3Dmodel_Reconstruction.ipynb in Google Colab, drop a video into inputs/, and run the cells top to bottom. It expects a CUDA runtime; a free T4 is enough.
